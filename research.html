Why disinformation worked in 2022.

My BA (Hons) Politics dissertation at Queen Mary University of London: how disinformation, voter predispositions and contested historical memory converged to shape voter perceptions in the 2022 Philippine presidential election.

Dissertation, 2026 Queen Mary University of London POL318 Research Project About 11,000 words Qualitative single-case study

The question

How did social media disinformation interact with existing political attitudes and historical narratives to shape voter perceptions during the 2022 Philippine presidential election?

Abstract

This dissertation examines how disinformation, voter predispositions, and contested historical memory interacted to shape voter perceptions during the 2022 Philippine presidential election, in which Ferdinand Marcos Jr. won with over 31 million votes and 59 per cent of the vote share, returning the Marcos family to the presidency three decades after the EDSA People Power Revolution. Existing scholarship has tended to treat digital disinformation, structural political factors, and memory politics as connected but insufficiently integrated processes, leaving open the question of how these dimensions worked together to produce the electoral outcome.

To address this gap, the dissertation constructs an original analytical framework, termed the convergence model, drawing on three theoretical pillars: narrative competition over interpretive authority, networked disinformation as a professionalised production system, and the populist logic of equivalence applied to memory politics. The model is operationalised through a qualitative single-case study design, employing thematic discourse analysis of social media content from Facebook, YouTube, and TikTok during the January to May 2022 campaign period. A two-layer coding scheme captures both narrative type (authoritarian nostalgia, conspiracy, strongman leadership, democratic disillusionment) and affective strategy. The analysis is triangulated against secondary quantitative evidence from Kasuya et al. (2024), Mendoza et al. (2025), and Dulay et al. (2023).

The dissertation argues that disinformation functioned not as an autonomous persuasive force but as a conditional catalyst, becoming politically consequential where it aligned with prior political commitments and contested historical memory. The experimental finding that one-shot exposure could not increase Marcos support is reinterpreted as confirming this logic. The findings contribute to comparative debates on democratic resilience and digital autocratisation, demonstrating that disinformation's electoral influence depends on structural and mnemonic conditions rather than on the inherent persuasiveness of false claims.

The convergence model

The model's claim is that voter perceptions in 2022 were a product of three processes working together, not of any one in isolation. It rests on three theoretical pillars.

Narrative competition

Campaigns compete for interpretive authority: whose account of the past and present counts as common sense.

Networked disinformation

Disinformation as a professionalised production system, not a scatter of individual false claims.

Memory politics

The populist logic of equivalence applied to collective memory, unifying diverse grievances into one coherent demand.

What it found

  • Disinformation operated as a conditional catalyst. It did not manufacture attitudes from nothing, nor merely reflect preferences that would have existed anyway; it drew its force from alignment with prior political commitments and contested historical memory.
  • The two positions in the literature, that disinformation was decisive and that structural forces made it secondary, are interacting explanations, not competing ones. Disinformation activated and intensified predispositions and gave them political form through platformed memory politics; predispositions set the conditions under which particular narratives became credible, socially validated and actionable.
  • Fact-checking in the Kasuya et al. (2024) experiment corrected belief in specific claims but failed to shift underlying attitudes towards the Marcos family. Correcting individual falsehoods does not address the predispositional and mnemonic conditions that make disinformation persuasive, so democratic resilience needs demand-side work, on institutional distrust, democratic fatigue and unresolved historical conflict, not only supply-side measures such as platform regulation, fact-checking and media literacy.
  • The case is consistent with digital autocratisation as described by Arugay and Mendoza (2024): the erosion of democratic norms through the normalisation of illiberal narratives inside democratic elections, most dangerous where post-authoritarian settlements remain contested.

Method

A qualitative single-case study using thematic discourse analysis of Facebook, YouTube and TikTok content from the January to May 2022 campaign period. Each item was coded on two layers: narrative type (authoritarian nostalgia, conspiracy, strongman leadership, democratic disillusionment) and affective strategy. The discourse analysis was triangulated against secondary quantitative evidence from Kasuya et al. (2024), Mendoza et al. (2025) and Dulay et al. (2023), and the model was evaluated against the structural and institutional explanations in the existing literature.

How it was written

I used AI tools to triage and summarise the research literature across every chapter and to stress-test the convergence model against counter-arguments. That compressed the early research and ideas stage by roughly 40 per cent. Every source, quotation and figure was verified manually, and the argument, coding and analysis are my own.
