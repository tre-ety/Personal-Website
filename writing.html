The ticket is the product's memory

28 October 20263 min readProduct

In my first two months as a product owner I wrote more than 200 Jira tickets for one shared development team that serves four departments. That number is not a boast; it is the shape of the job. Sales, operations, finance and HR all raise requests directly, and the only thing that stops those requests from colliding is a backlog that remembers what each one means.

So every story I hand over carries the same four parts. A user story, so the team knows who this is for and why. Given/When/Then acceptance criteria, so "done" is a test rather than an opinion. What is out of scope, written down, because scope creep rarely arrives as a decision; it arrives as a reasonable-sounding assumption. And the cross-functional dependencies, so the finance rule that affects the operations screen is visible before anyone builds the screen.

Big pieces get split into sub-tasks the developers can actually estimate. One module redesign became around twenty sub-tasks; another became twelve. The split is not bureaucracy. It is the difference between a plan the team can commit to and a wish.

The payoff is that the ticket outlives the conversation. When someone asks in six weeks why a rule works the way it does, the answer is in the ticket, with the people who agreed it and the things they chose not to do. A backlog written this way is the product's memory. A backlog written any other way is a to-do list that forgets.

Check the data before you describe the product

28 October 20262 min readProduct

The quickest way to write a bug into a product is to write a requirement that describes how you think it behaves today. Every ticket that says "currently, the system does X" is a claim, and if the claim is wrong, the developer will faithfully build the change on top of a fiction.

So before a ticket describes current behaviour, I query the product's own database and look. It takes minutes. It has caught a dashboard whose two panels disagreed with each other before the dashboard went out, and it has turned several "fixes" into "that already works; the problem is somewhere else".

The same habit applies to code. At CIRQL, an early-stage app built in Flutter, Dart and Firebase, I read the codebase with AI assistance before scoping features. It took about a working day out of each scoping round, and it meant I could argue feasibility from what the code did rather than from what I assumed it did. Product owners do not need to write the code. They do need to know what it does.

The same principle, applied to research rather than software, is why every figure in my dissertation was verified by hand even where AI had done the first pass.

What organising taught me about product

28 October 20263 min readOrganising

Before product, I organised. With Citizens UK in East London I worked on a portfolio of asks at once: the Living Wage, Living Hours, temporary accommodation, English language provision, air quality and youth safety, across an alliance of faith institutions, schools, universities, trade unions, councils and grassroots groups. It is the closest thing I know to running a roadmap for a dozen stakeholders who did not choose each other.

Three things carried over directly. The ask. In organising, an ask is specific, time-bound and put to a named person who can say yes. "Pay the Living Wage" is a slogan; "Will you commit, by this date, to accredit?" is an ask. A good user story has the same discipline: one change, one owner, one test of done.

Turnout is a metric. For the TELCO and National Delegates Assemblies I managed turnout strategy and delegate tracking, with CRM-style records of who had committed and who had shown up. Treating participation as a number you track, rather than a hope, is exactly what release testing with real users is in product: you count who turned up, not who said they would.

Accountability is a demo. The V&A Living Wage campaign ended with student testimony we scripted, filmed and edited, carried into a formal submission to the Mayor of London at Tower Hamlets Town Hall. A public moment with a decision-maker in the room is the organiser's sprint review. You bring evidence, you ask for a commitment, and you find out whether the work held.

The full story is in the Citizens UK case study.

AI for speed, people for judgement

28 October 20263 min readWorking with AI

I use AI tools every day, and I have the numbers for what they save. Triaging and summarising the literature for my dissertation compressed the early research stage by about 40 per cent. Turning Citizens UK policy material into posters and adverts went about 25 per cent faster. Drafting landing-page copy at CIRQL took roughly half the production time of doing it by hand, at a lower cost than an equivalent commercial tool.

Every one of those figures comes with a condition. The dissertation figures were verified manually against the sources. The campaign materials kept a manual review for audience fit, because an advert that reads well to a model and wrong to a parent in Tower Hamlets has failed. The CIRQL copy went through five revision passes precisely because the first drafts contained AI-introduced errors: inaccurate image generation and phrasing aimed at the wrong reader.

The rule I work to is simple. AI compresses the work of producing options. People decide, and people stay accountable. That is why, when a request came in to let an AI assistant learn from customer call transcripts, the pilot was scoped with legal and data-protection review before a single transcript was used, and with deflection measured against a real baseline rather than assumed.

Speed without judgement is just a faster way to be wrong. Judgement without speed is a slower way to be right. The job is to have both, and to be honest about which one the machine is supplying.
