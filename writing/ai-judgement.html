AI for speed, people for judgement

28 October 20261 min readWorking with AI

I use AI tools every day, and I have the numbers for what they save. Triaging and summarising the literature for my dissertation compressed the early research stage by about 40 per cent. Turning Citizens UK policy material into posters and adverts went about 25 per cent faster. Drafting landing-page copy at CIRQL took roughly half the production time of doing it by hand, at a lower cost than an equivalent commercial tool.

Every one of those figures comes with a condition. The dissertation figures were verified manually against the sources. The campaign materials kept a manual review for audience fit, because an advert that reads well to a model and wrong to a parent in Tower Hamlets has failed. The CIRQL copy went through five revision passes precisely because the first drafts contained AI-introduced errors: inaccurate image generation and phrasing aimed at the wrong reader.

The rule I work to is simple. AI compresses the work of producing options. People decide, and people stay accountable. That is why, when a request came in to let an AI assistant learn from customer call transcripts, the pilot was scoped with legal and data-protection review before a single transcript was used, and with deflection measured against a real baseline rather than assumed.

Speed without judgement is just a faster way to be wrong. Judgement without speed is a slower way to be right. The job is to have both, and to be honest about which one the machine is supplying.
